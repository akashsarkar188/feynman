## Summary
This document presents a literature review exploring the historical evolution of dominant values in European land-water interaction, spanning from the early modern period to the present. The review identifies four key phases: pre-19th century multi-functional local uses, 19th-century state-led control and commodification, 20th-century drainage and agricultural intensification, and late 20th/21st-century ecological integration. It also discusses the roles of key actors, scientific and technological influences, regional variations, and the emergence of ecological values, concluding with consensus findings, inferred disagreements, and open research questions. The review is based on an English-language corpus, and explicitly notes limitations due to restricted access to the full text of several cited sources.

## Strengths
- **[S1] Transparent Reporting of Limitations:** The review is commendable for its explicit "Evidence caveats" section and the "Verification Note," which clearly state the accessibility issues of several cited sources. This transparency is crucial for assessing the reliability of the claims made.
- **[S2] Clear Periodization Framework:** The proposed four-phase historical periodization provides a structured and understandable narrative for the complex evolution of land-water values in Europe. The accompanying mermaid graph is a helpful visual aid.
- **[S3] Comprehensive Thematic Coverage:** Beyond historical phases, the review systematically covers key aspects like actors, science/technology, regional variations, and ecological values, offering a multi-faceted perspective on the topic.

## Weaknesses
- **[W1] FATAL: Over-reliance on Abstract-Only Sources for Core Claims:** A significant portion (7 out of 13) of the cited sources were accessed only via abstract, metadata, or limited excerpts. Many core claims and "consensus findings" within the review, particularly for Phases 2 and 4, and the "Consensus findings" section itself, heavily rely on these inaccessible full texts. For a literature review, making definitive statements or identifying "key concepts" based solely on abstracts fundamentally compromises its empirical rigor and the verifiability of its arguments. This makes the review's conclusions highly speculative.
- **[W2] FATAL: Flawed Methodology for "Disagreements and Historiographic Debates":** The section explicitly titles itself as "Disagreements and historiographic debates (inferred from corpus gaps)." This approach is methodologically unsound for a literature review, which should identify *actual* debates and disagreements present *within* the reviewed literature, rather than inferring them from what the current (limited) corpus *lacks*. This section effectively speculates on research gaps rather than reporting on existing academic discourse.
- **[W3] MAJOR: Inconsistent Strength of Claims Despite Caveats:** While the review acknowledges the limitations of abstract-only sources, it frequently uses strong, definitive language for claims primarily supported by these very sources. For example, statements about "universalization," "dominance," and "inherent conflict" are presented with high certainty, even when explicitly attributed to abstracts, creating a tension between the stated caution and the declarative tone.
- **[W4] MINOR: Language Bias Limitation Understated:** The search strategy specifies an "English-language peer-reviewed papers" corpus. While practical, for a topic like "European" land-water interaction, this implicitly biases the review towards Anglophone scholarship, potentially overlooking significant research, diverse perspectives, and different value systems articulated in other European languages. This limitation should be more prominently discussed in the "Open questions and research gaps" or "Consensus findings" sections, as it impacts the representativeness of the findings.

## Questions for Authors

Would the primary claims remain the same if full-text access was unavailable, or would they be rephrased as hypotheses or observations for future research?
- **[Q2]** Could the authors elaborate on how the identified "consensus findings" were established, particularly when some "key concepts" are explicitly noted as originating from limited-access sources? What threshold of full-text support was considered sufficient for a "consensus" claim?

## Verdict
This review presents a clear and well-structured overview of a complex topic, exhibiting commendable transparency regarding its source limitations. However, the pervasive reliance on abstract-only sources for foundational claims and the methodological flaw in identifying historiographic debates constitute fatal weaknesses.

**Confidence Score:** 2/5 (Low confidence in the robustness of several core conclusions due to evidence limitations).

**Would this pass at [venue]?** Unlikely to pass at a selective academic venue (e.g., a journal specializing in environmental history or policy) in its current form. The fatal weaknesses regarding evidence and methodology would likely lead to outright rejection or a request for major revisions requiring substantial re-evaluation of the evidence base. For a less formal setting (e.g., an internal report or a preliminary white paper), it might pass, but with strong caveats appended to its findings.
