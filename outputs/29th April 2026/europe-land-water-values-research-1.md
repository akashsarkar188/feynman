### 2. Historical Periodization of Dominant Values

The values guiding land-water interaction in Europe have undergone significant transformations from the early modern period to the present, moving from diverse, localized appreciations of "waters" to a more utilitarian, commodity-driven view, and then gradually incorporating environmental and ecological considerations.

*   **Pre-19th Century (Early Modern to early Industrial Age): Diverse and Multi-functional Values.**
    *   Prior to the 19th century, water was often viewed with an "empiricist view of waters as many," recognizing its diverse characteristics shaped by natural philosophy, folklore, and religion [8].
    *   In regions like Munich, water served multiple functions including drinking, commerce, waste disposal, defense, transport, and hydropower [4].
    *   In Venice and Holland, water management was focused on exploitation for agricultural production and land reclamation through marshland conversion [3]. Water infrastructure often reflected an equilibrium between society and its environment, rooted in empirical practices and local technical culture within "hydraulic communities" [5].
    *   Land management was characterized by an "Era of Peasantry," implying traditional, agrarian-focused values [6].
*   **19th Century: Commodification, Control, and Utilitarianism.**
    *   The 19th century saw a shift to an "essentialist view" of water as a commodity, primarily valued for its utility and quantity, with concerns focused on "undesirable impurities" [8].
    *   "Administrative states" promoted centralized control and exploitation of water resources, often contrasting with traditional local management [5].
    *   Industrialization and urbanization, as seen in Munich, led to increased pollution. Water's functions were gradually replaced by new technologies like electricity and railroads, leading to the disappearance of traditional water networks as it became a problem to be managed or removed [4].
    *   Land management moved through "Innovations and Rights" and "Intensification" regimes, signaling a drive towards greater agricultural productivity [6].
*   **20th Century: Industrialization, Drainage Paradigm, and Emergence of Challenges.**
    *   The early to mid-20th century was dominated by an "Industrialization" regime in land management, further driving agricultural expansion [6].
    *   A "drainage paradigm" was prominent, especially in 20th-century rural Europe, where state bureaucracies valued converting "unused" wetlands to arable land and creating new land from the sea for agricultural productivity [2].
    *   However, challenges to this paradigm began to emerge in the "last third of the twentieth century," driven by scientists, environmental activists, and citizens scrutinizing the environmental impact of such interventions [2].
*   **Late 20th Century to Present: Ecological Awareness and Multifaceted Valuation.**
    *   The late 20th and early 21st centuries have seen a progression towards recognizing and integrating ecological values. Contemporary water management acknowledges three archetypal perspectives: engineering control, market-based mechanisms, and "working with natural water ecosystems" [7].
    *   There is a re-emerging appreciation for the "qualitative diversity of waters" and their environmental quality, moving beyond a purely utilitarian view [8].
    *   The emphasis shifts towards a "valuing water" paradigm that considers not just uses and benefits, but also broader personal guiding principles and governance-related values [7].

### 3. Key Actors and Their Value Perspectives

*   **Early Modern Period:**
    *   **Local Communities/Peasants:** Valued water for diverse functions (drinking, transport, agriculture, defense) and managed it through empirical practices, fostering an "equilibrium between society and its environment" within "hydraulic communities" [4, 5].
    *   **Landowners/Entrepreneurs (Venice, Holland):** Drove land reclamation and water exploitation for increased agricultural production [3].
    *   **Bavarian Duke/Citizens (Munich):** Their conflicts and negotiations influenced the development of water networks [4].
*   **19th Century:**
    *   **Agronomists, Engineers, and Lawyers:** Some advocated for "hydraulic societies" and local, historical understanding of water management (e.g., François-Jacques Jaubert de Passa, Carlo Cattaneo), contrasting with centralized state approaches [5].
    *   **State Engineers/Administrative States:** Promoted a "naturalized vision of water" to support state claims on resources, emphasizing centralized control and exploitation for state objectives and industrial needs [5].
    *   **Industrialists/Urban Planners:** Valued water for waste discharge and hydropower in early industrial settings, later seeking to replace river functions with new technologies as pollution increased [4].
*   **20th Century:**
    *   **State Bureaucracies:** Actively designed and implemented large-scale land drainage and reclamation schemes, driven by values of agricultural productivity and economic development (the "drainage paradigm") [2].
    *   **Scientists, Environmental Activists, and Citizens (from late 20th C):** Began to challenge the dominant drainage paradigm, scrutinizing its environmental and social impacts, and advocating for the protection of existing socio-ecological environments [2].
    *   **Water Professionals/Policymakers (Contemporary):** Engaged in balancing engineering, market-based, and ecosystem-focused approaches, reflecting a diverse set of values including economic efficiency, social justice, and environmental sustainability [7].

### 4. Influence of Science/Technology

Science and technology have consistently been intertwined with the evolution of values in land-water interactions:

*   **Early Modern to 19th Century:**
    *   **Hydraulic Technologies:** Early hydraulic technologies like artificial canals ("Stadtbäche"), dams, weirs, and irrigation systems were crucial for managing water for multiple purposes (e.g., transport, hydropower, agriculture) [4, 3].
    *   **Local Technical Culture:** "Hydraulic communities" developed empirical practices and technical cultures for water management, contrasting with later state-led engineering [5].
    *   **Commodification of Water:** The "discovery of its compound nature in the 18th century" contributed to a conceptual shift, facilitating the view of water as a uniform commodity, further reinforced by industrial processes that introduced "impurities" [8].
*   **19th Century and Industrialization:**
    *   **State-led Engineering:** "Administrative states" employed engineering to assert control over water resources, often for large-scale projects aligned with state objectives [5].
    *   **Replacement Technologies:** New industrial technologies such as electricity and railroads reduced the functional reliance on rivers for transport and power, leading to the abandonment and covering of traditional water networks in urban centers like Munich [4].
*   **20th Century: Large-Scale Interventions:**
    *   **Drainage and Reclamation:** Sophisticated engineering techniques were employed in large-scale land drainage and reclamation projects to convert wetlands and create new land, driven by agricultural and economic goals [2].
*   **Contemporary: Integrated Approaches:**
    *   **Diverse Management Tools:** Modern water management incorporates advanced engineering solutions, market-based mechanisms (implying economic modeling and policy tools), and scientific understanding of ecosystems for nature-based solutions [7].
    *   **Influence on Public Perception:** Technological advancements and their perceived impact continue to shape public assessment and valuation of water [8].

### 5. Regional Variations

Significant regional variations are evident across Europe in the history of land-water interaction values:

*   **Venice and Holland:** These regions are highlighted as prime examples of societies "built on water," mastering marshland conversion (Dutch polders, Venetian lagoon) for agricultural production [3]. They developed advanced hydraulic technologies that influenced other European countries [3].
*   **Munich (Germany):** Provides a specific case study of urban water networks, showcasing a transition from a multi-functional water system integrated into city life to one marginalized by industrialization and pollution [4].
*   **France and Italy:** In the 19th century, these countries witnessed a tension between the values and practices of local "hydraulic communities" and the centralized control sought by "administrative states" [5].
*   **General European Land-Management Regimes:** While common regimes like "Era of Peasantry" and "Industrialization" are identified, "marked spatial heterogeneity" in land-management regimes existed across Europe until around 1950 [6]. This suggests diverse local adaptations and timings of transitions.

### 6. Emergence of Ecological Values

The emergence of ecological values marks a significant shift in European land-water interactions:

*   **Early Awareness:** Even in the early modern period, "contemporaries" raised questions about the "environmental impact of agricultural progress" [3]. This indicates an nascent, though perhaps not formalized, recognition of human-induced environmental change.
*   **Late 20th Century Challenge to Drainage Paradigm:** A clear emergence of ecological values occurred in the "last third of the twentieth century." Scientists, environmental activists, and citizens began to "scrutinize such interventions" as large-scale land reclamation and drainage, effectively challenging the dominant "drainage paradigm" and advocating for the protection of socio-ecological environments like wetlands [2].
*   **Contemporary Paradigm Shift:** The concept of "valuing water" has evolved to include "working with natural water ecosystems" as a distinct and increasingly important management perspective, alongside engineering and market-based approaches [7]. This reflects a more holistic appreciation of water's role in supporting ecosystems.
*   **Re-appreciation of Qualitative Diversity:** There is a contemporary re-awakening of interest in "water taste and water quality" and the "qualitative diversity of waters." This re-appreciation is seen as potentially fostering a greater willingness to invest in "environmental quality generally," thereby linking qualitative values to broader ecological concerns [8].
