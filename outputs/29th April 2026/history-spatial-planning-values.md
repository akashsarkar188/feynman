# Values in the History of Spatial Planning: Literature Review (Provisional, Source-Bounded)

## Executive summary
This review synthesizes a **selected, partly access-limited canon** on values in planning history/theory/historiography. In this bounded corpus, a recurring pattern is visible: critiques of technocratic-rational planning are followed by stronger emphasis on pluralism, deliberation, justice, and postcolonial/multicultural historiography [1][3][4][7][9][12][14].

Because some core items were only available through metadata/landing pages in this run, claims are expressed as **recurring patterns in selected sources**, not field-wide prevalence estimates.

## Scope and method
- **Topic focus:** normative values in the historical development of spatial planning thought and historiography.
- **Corpus construction:** canonical texts + key critiques identified in delegated triage (`outputs/history-spatial-planning-values-research-1.md`).
- **Time scope:** foundational 20th-century planning theory anchors through recent historiographic work.
- **Method:** qualitative narrative synthesis with explicit confidence and access-status caveats.

### Terminology boundary
- **Planning theory:** normative/epistemic models of how planning should work.
- **Planning history:** historical trajectories of ideas/practices.
- **Planning historiography:** how those histories are written and contested.

## Historical value trajectories (in this corpus)

### Trajectory A: Rational-technical confidence to epistemic humility
Selected canonical works indicate movement away from assumptions of single-objective optimization toward acceptance of bounded rationality, plural interests, and problem indeterminacy [1][2][3].

### Trajectory B: Procedure and legitimacy
In this corpus, communicative/collaborative planning literature ties legitimacy to deliberation, inclusion, and institutional process quality [5][6]. **Status:** provisional in this run because both key anchors were not fully read end-to-end.

### Trajectory C: Justice, recognition, and historical redress
Critical planning and historiographic works foreground exclusion, control, and representational erasure, pushing value frameworks toward redistribution, recognition, and epistemic pluralization [7][8][12][13][14].

## Recurring value conflicts (selected sources)
1. Expert authority vs democratic pluralism [1][2][6].
2. Growth/efficiency vs justice/equity [4][12].
3. Consensus-oriented governance vs conflict/power-aware critique [6][8][12].
4. Universal planning narratives vs situated/postcolonial histories [7][9][13][14].

## Recurring patterns (not field-wide consensus)
1. Canonical anti-technocratic critiques are repeatedly cited in planning theory histories [1][2][3][4].
2. Participatory/deliberative legitimacy becomes more central in influential late-20th-century planning theory discussions [5][6].
3. Justice and historiographic critique widen what “good planning” evaluates and whose experiences count [7][12][13][14].

## Disagreements and historiographic debates
1. **Process vs outcomes:** participatory legitimacy vs distributive-material outcomes [6][12].
2. **Consensus vs agonism:** agreement-seeking vs explicit institutionalization of conflict [6][8].
3. **Canon vs plural histories:** integrative canon-building vs de-centering Euro/settler narratives [7][9][13][14].
4. **State capacity vs state critique:** planning-as-public-capacity vs planning-as-discipline/control [4][8].
