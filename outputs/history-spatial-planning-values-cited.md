# Values in the History of Spatial Planning: A Literature Review

## Executive summary
In the reviewed corpus, the history of spatial planning can be read as a sequence of value re-orderings: from public-order and expert-rational ideals toward pluralism, participation, justice, and postcolonial critique [4][7][9][11][12][13][14]. The strongest evidence in this run supports three broad claims: (1) mid-20th century rational-comprehensive planning was substantially challenged by incrementalist, advocacy, and wicked-problem critiques [1][2][3]; (2) communicative/collaborative planning recentered deliberative legitimacy [5][6]; and (3) justice, multicultural, and postcolonial perspectives increasingly re-framed what counts as “good” planning and whose histories are recognized [7][8][12][13][14].

Because several canonical texts were only bibliographically/partially accessed in this run, field-wide prevalence claims are treated cautiously [2][5][6][11][15][16].

## Periodization: value shifts in planning history

### Phase 1: Reformist and ordering values
Early planning traditions are commonly associated with order, sanitation/public health, and civic improvement (inference from planning history syntheses) [9][11].

### Phase 2: Rational-comprehensive confidence
Mid-century planning theory emphasized expertise, coherence, and forecast-led coordination, with strong confidence in technical reason and state steering [2][4][11].

### Phase 3: Critique of synoptic rationalism (1959–1970s)
Incrementalism, advocacy pluralism, and wicked-problems critiques challenged the assumption that planners could define a single optimal public interest [1][2][3].

### Phase 4: Communicative/collaborative turn (1990s)
Planning legitimacy increasingly tied to deliberation, stakeholder inclusion, and institution-building across fragmented governance arenas [5][6].

### Phase 5: Justice/postcolonial reframing (late 1990s–present)
Critical scholarship foregrounded inequity, exclusion, social control, and coloniality in both planning practice and planning historiography [7][8][12][13][14].

## Planning traditions and value orientations
- **Rational-comprehensive**: coherence, technical expertise, predictability [2][3][4].
- **Incrementalist**: feasibility, bounded rationality, adaptation [2].
- **Advocacy/pluralist**: representation of multiple publics, procedural fairness [1].
- **Communicative/collaborative**: dialogue, negotiated legitimacy, relational capacity [5][6].
- **Critical justice/postcolonial**: redistribution, recognition, anti-oppression, epistemic pluralization [7][8][12][13][14].

## Recurring value conflicts
1. Expert authority vs democratic pluralism [1][2][6].
2. Growth/efficiency vs equity/justice [4][12].
3. Strategic coherence vs local knowledge/everyday urban life [11][16].
4. Consensus orientation vs power-aware agonistic critique [6][8][12].
5. Universal planning narratives vs situated/postcolonial histories [7][9][13][14].

## Consensus (bounded to this corpus)
1. Canonical critiques (incrementalist, advocacy, wicked problems) are central to the decline of confidence in purely technocratic planning [1][2][3].
2. Communicative/collaborative planning reframed legitimacy around process and participation [5][6].
3. Late-20th and contemporary scholarship expanded value vocabularies toward justice, recognition, and historical redress [7][8][12][13][14].

## Disagreements and historiographic debates
1. **Process vs outcomes**: whether participatory legitimacy is sufficient without distributive gains [6][12].
2. **Consensus vs conflict**: whether planning should seek common ground or explicitly institutionalize enduring antagonism [6][8].
3. **Universalism vs situated histories**: whether a shared planning canon is useful or structurally exclusionary [7][9][13][14].
4. **State capacity vs anti-statist skepticism**: whether strong public planning is emancipatory or often disciplinary [4][8].

## Open questions
1. Which value frameworks have most clearly improved distributive outcomes (not only deliberative process quality)?
2. How should planning history better integrate non-Anglophone and non-settler-centric lineages?
3. What methods best compare value change across eras (intellectual history vs bibliometrics vs policy tracing)?
4. Where do climate-transition planning values align/conflict with older justice and participation commitments?

## Suggested follow-up reading
- Davidoff (1965), Lindblom (1959), Rittel & Webber (1973) for critique of technocratic planning foundations [1][2][3].
- Friedmann (1987), Healey (1996, 1997) for planning theory transitions [4][5][6].
- Sandercock (1998), Yiftachel (1998), Fainstein (2010), and newer planning-history handbooks for justice/critical historiography [7][8][9][10][12][14].
- Faludi (1973), Hall (*Cities of Tomorrow*), and Jacobs (1961) for additional canon and counter-canon context [11][15][16].
