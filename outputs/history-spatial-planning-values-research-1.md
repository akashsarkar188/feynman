## Periodization of value shifts (historically grounded)

1. **Proto-professional / reformist foundations (late 19th–early 20th c.)**: public health, order, civic improvement, and social reform become planning justifications (inference from canon histories and handbook framing) [9][11].

2. **High modernist / comprehensive-rational phase (mid-20th c.)**: faith in expert-led, synoptic, scientific planning and state capacity (inference) [2][4][11].

3. **Pluralist and anti-technocratic critique (1959–1970s)**: incrementalism, advocacy, and wicked-problem arguments undermine single-objective rationalism [1][2][3].

4. **Communicative/collaborative turn (1990s)**: legitimacy shifts toward deliberation, participation, and negotiated strategy in fragmented societies [5][6].

5. **Justice, multicultural, and postcolonial critique (late 1990s–present)**: explicit focus on exclusion, social control, race/coloniality, and equity in both planning practice and planning historiography [7][8][12][13][14].

---

## Planning traditions and value orientations

- **Rational-comprehensive tradition** → coherence, expertise, predictability, instrumental reason (later challenged) [2][3][4].
- **Incrementalist tradition** → feasibility, bounded rationality, adjustment over optimization [2].
- **Advocacy/pluralist tradition** → representation of multiple publics, adversarial-democratic legitimacy [1].
- **Communicative/collaborative tradition** → dialogue, consensus-seeking (or workable agreement), relational/institutional capacity [5][6].
- **Critical/postmodern/postcolonial justice tradition** → recognition, redistribution, anti-oppression, epistemic de-centering of Eurocentric canon [7][8][12][13][14].

---

## Recurring value conflicts in planning history

1. **Expert authority vs democratic pluralism** [1][2][6].
2. **Efficiency/growth vs justice/equity** [4][12].
3. **State coordination vs everyday urban life/local knowledge** (seen in anti-modernist critiques; inference) [11][16].
4. **Consensus-seeking vs agonistic/power-aware conflict** [6][8][12].
5. **Universalist planning narratives vs situated/colonial histories** [7][9][13][14].
