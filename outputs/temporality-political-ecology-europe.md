# Literature Review: Temporality from a Political Ecology Perspective in Europe

**Scope:** Europe-focused literature on temporality in political ecology, planning, governance, climate, and socio-environmental transformation (2015–2026; older foundations included where essential).

## 1) Executive summary

Across the literature reviewed, **temporality is not treated as a neutral backdrop**. It is a political relation that structures what counts as urgency, delay, feasibility, restoration, adaptation, and justice. In European cases, temporality is most visible in four arenas: **planning reform, climate and energy transition governance, infrastructure/permit regimes, and climate justice**. The strongest shared finding is that time is a site of power: actors use speed, delay, waiting, deadlines, and future horizons to authorize some futures and foreclose others.

A second finding is that the literature is increasingly moving from abstract claims about “time” to **operational questions**: who controls schedules, which temporal horizons are built into plans, how long harms last, how institutions synchronize or desynchronize conflicting temporalities, and which groups absorb the costs of temporal mismatch. This makes temporality usable as an analytical bridge between political ecology, planning studies, and governance research.

A third finding is that the **European literature is fragmented but convergent**. Political ecology texts on extraction, conservation, borders, and climate justice converge with planning and governance studies on acceleration, procedural delay, anticipatory governance, and adaptation justice. However, few studies explicitly call themselves political ecology while working on temporality in Europe. The concept is therefore present, but distributed across adjacent fields.

## 2) Scope and approach

### Key questions
- How has temporality been theorized in political ecology since 2015, and what foundational works remain necessary?
- How are climate governance, environmental change, and planning temporalized in Europe?
- How do slow violence, urgency, crisis, transition, anticipation, and delay interact?
- What is temporal justice, and how do uneven futures emerge?
- How do infrastructure, permits, landscapes, and resource governance produce multiple temporalities?
- How can temporality be operationalized in research on planning and governance?

### Source base used here
- **Peer-reviewed articles and book chapters** from 2015 onward, prioritizing Europe or Europe-relevant cases.
- **Major European institutional reports** on justice, adaptation, and foresight.
- **Foundational pre-2015 works** where they are clearly influential for the temporality debate.

### Method note
This review is a **curated literature synthesis**, not an exhaustive systematic review. It emphasizes source texts with explicit discussion of time, temporality, speed, delay, urgency, horizons, or temporal justice.

## 3) Core conceptual findings

### 3.1 Temporality in political ecology: from background condition to analytic object
Political ecology has long examined uneven power in space; the recent literature insists that **time is equally political**. In this view, temporality includes:
- **duration** and persistence of harms,
- **speed** and acceleration,
- **delay** and waiting,
- **sequence** and timing,
- **anticipation** and projection,
- **memory** and historical responsibility,
- **futurity** and long-term imaginaries,
- **synchronization/desynchronization** between institutions, ecologies, and social groups.

Nustad’s article on conservation in South Africa shows how protected areas are often justified through claims of timeless nature, while in fact they depend on historically produced exclusions and temporal contradictions (Nustad 2020). Fent and Kojola’s special section argues that extractive conflicts are organized through histories, memories, velocities, delays, and epistemologies of time rather than only through territory or resources (Fent & Kojola 2020). Kolinjivadi, Vela Almeida, and Martineau (2020) critique the use of linear clock-time and limits-style framings in climate politics, arguing that capitalist modernity imposes a homogeneous time that obscures emergent and non-linear ecological change.

### 3.2 Climate governance and environmental change are temporal struggles
The literature repeatedly shows that climate governance is never only about targets; it is also about **which time horizon counts**. Marquardt and Delina (2021) argue that energy and climate studies need to interrogate the politics of time-making in transitions research. The issue is not simply how fast decarbonization should happen, but who defines the pace, sequence, and acceptable delay.

European governance documents reinforce this temporal framing. The EEA briefing on justice in sustainability transitions stresses that transitions are long-term structural changes affecting current and future generations, and that justice requires attention to distribution, procedure, recognition, restoration, and temporality (EEA 2024). The European Commission’s Strategic Foresight Report likewise frames governance as resilience-building under uncertainty, emphasizing preparedness and long-term anticipation (European Commission 2025).

### 3.3 Slow violence, crisis, urgency, and transition temporalities
A recurring tension is between **slow violence** and **urgent crisis**. Slow violence names the diffuse, delayed, and often invisible harms produced by environmental change and extractive regimes. In Europe-facing work, Schindel (2022) shows how the EU border regime produces slow violence that is obscured by spectacle; Widengård (2024) shows how environmental permits become tools for synchronizing contested temporalities in Sweden’s fuel transition; and the German coal mine planning literature shows how waiting itself becomes a political condition that organizes protest and governance (Müller 2019).

At the same time, climate politics often mobilizes urgency to justify acceleration. The literature is skeptical of simply treating speed as inherently progressive. In planning, “speeding up” can reduce participation, strengthen property rights, and advance regressive agendas (Cerrada Morato et al. 2025). Thus, urgency is not neutral: it can be a tool of both mobilization and depoliticization.

### 3.4 Temporal justice and uneven socio-environmental futures
Temporal justice is emerging as a useful concept for naming how past harms, present burdens, and future risks are distributed unevenly. Bopp and Bercht (2021) explicitly treat temporality as a dimension of climate justice, arguing that justice concerns the interrelation of past, present, and future, the momentum of time, and which kinds of time are recognized. The EEA similarly includes temporality as one of the five justice concepts relevant to sustainability transitions, alongside intersectionality, capabilities, epistemology, and spatiality (EEA 2024).

This matters for Europe because climate and transition policies often shift costs onto regions and groups with less power: coal regions, borderlands, rural peripheries, low-income urban neighborhoods, and future generations. The literature on just relocation, adaptation, and restoration increasingly frames justice as temporal: who pays for historical degradation, who benefits from restoration later, and who is forced to endure transition waiting times now.

### 3.5 Infrastructure, landscapes, and resource governance as temporal devices
Infrastructure studies provide some of the clearest empirical work on political time. Marshall and Cowell (2016) show how major infrastructure planning is driven by a “command of time” that privileges speed and linear project sequences. Widengård (2024) extends this by showing that permits are themselves temporal forms of control, not merely administrative steps. Planning literature in London and across Europe similarly shows that temporal reforms are not simply technical fixes but deeply political devices that reshape participation and rights (Raco et al. 2018; Cerrada Morato et al. 2025).

In resource governance, temporality appears through **waiting, anticipation, delay, lock-in, and phase-out**. Fent and Kojola (2020) bring together resource temporalities and temporal strategies around extraction. The coal-planning case in Germany shows how villagers experience a prolonged stage of waiting and how protest can be reconfigured through de-synchronization (Müller 2019). In European transition settings, the timing of permits, phase-outs, and restoration plans matters as much as the formal policy target.

### 3.6 Multi-scalar and cross-border temporalities
The European literature shows that temporality is multi-scalar: local, national, EU, and transboundary timeframes often conflict. Watershed governance, energy networks, border regimes, and regional planning all require synchronization across different temporal scales. The multi-scalar water governance literature on the EU Water Framework Directive is a foundational example: hydrological time and administrative time do not align cleanly (Hüesker & Moss 2015). More recent border and infrastructure studies show that cross-border systems create temporal asymmetries by shifting risks, costs, and delays across borders and jurisdictions.

### 3.7 Adaptive, anticipatory, and contested governance approaches
European governance increasingly relies on anticipatory tools: strategic foresight, adaptation planning, scenario-building, and resilience frameworks. But the literature warns that anticipation can become a way to stabilize existing power relations unless it is made contestable. The EEA’s just-sustainability framework stresses participation, but also notes that future generations and other species must be considered. Fünfgeld and Schmid (2020) argue that adaptation planning must be justice-sensitive because adaptation decisions will unfold over years and can reproduce existing inequalities. Anticipatory governance is therefore not just about prediction; it is about whose futures are made governable.

## 4) Key debates and recurring authors

### Recurring authors and clusters
- **Knut G. Nustad** — political ecology of time in conservation and heritage.
- **Ashley Fent & Erik Kojola** — extraction, memory, delay, anticipation, and temporal strategies.
- **Vijay Kolinjivadi, Diana Vela Almeida, Jonathan Martineau** — climate temporality, limits, and critique of linear clock-time.
- **Jens Marquardt & Laurence L. Delina** — time-making in energy and climate transitions.
- **Marie Widengård** — permit time and synchronization in infrastructure transition.
- **Judith Bopp & Anna Lena Bercht** — temporal justice in climate justice.
- **Hartmut Fünfgeld & Benedikt Schmid** — justice-sensitive adaptation planning.
- **Mike Raco, Daniel Durrant, Nicola Livingstone** — planning speed, urban politics, and temporalities.
- **Tim Marshall & Richard Cowell** — infrastructure planning and command of time.
- **Estela Schindel** — slow violence, border regimes, and European violence.

### Main debates
1. **Speed vs. slowness**: Is speed needed to address climate change, or does acceleration reproduce domination and exclusion?
2. **Urgency vs. deliberation**: Can urgent action coexist with democratic participation and justice?
3. **Linear vs. plural time**: Is the dominant model still clock-time and project time, or should politics account for multiple rhythms and deep time?
4. **Adaptation vs. transformation**: Are policies managing future risk, or transforming the conditions that produce temporal injustice?
5. **Operationalization**: How can temporality be measured or traced without reducing it to simple deadlines or lag indicators?
6. **Scale**: How do local experiences of waiting, loss, and restoration interact with EU-wide transitions and global climate timelines?

### Key conceptual frameworks
- **Slow violence**: delayed, diffuse harm that is politically obscured.
- **Temporal justice**: fairness across past, present, and future; attention to who waits, who bears transition costs, and who inherits damage.
- **Temporality as relational**: time is produced through interaction between institutions, ecologies, and social groups.
- **Timescapes / temporal governance**: planning and governance as temporal ordering devices.
- **Anticipatory governance**: policy organized around futures, scenarios, and preparedness.
- **Permit time / infrastructural time**: administrative procedures as temporal control.
- **Ecological or more-than-human time**: attention to nonhuman rhythms, restoration horizons, and deep time.

## 6) Definitions of temporality in this literature

The reviewed literature does not converge on one single definition, but several stable definitions recur:

1. **Temporality as plural time**: multiple rhythms, horizons, durations, and sequences coexist.
2. **Temporality as political**: time is produced, governed, and contested, not simply experienced.
3. **Temporality as relational**: time emerges through interactions among institutions, ecologies, technologies, and social groups.
4. **Temporality as justice-relevant**: who experiences delay, acceleration, waiting, or future risk matters ethically and politically.
5. **Temporality as operational**: it can be studied through policy schedules, permits, timelines, memory work, anticipation, and historical traces.

A compact working definition for this topic could be:

> **Temporality in political ecology is the contested organization of past, present, and future across social and ecological relations, including the politics of speed, delay, anticipation, memory, and long-term change.**

## 7) Methods for studying temporality in political ecology

The literature uses a fairly consistent methodological repertoire:

- **Ethnography / participant observation**: to trace lived experiences of waiting, urgency, adaptation, or conservation.
- **Comparative case studies**: especially for governance and planning reform across countries or regions.
- **Discourse analysis**: to examine how urgency, crisis, resilience, and future horizons are framed.
- **Historical and archival analysis**: useful for resource extraction, conservation, and infrastructure legacies.
- **Policy and legal analysis**: especially for permits, planning regimes, climate litigation, and foresight.
- **Mixed-methods and qualitative interviews**: for transition governance and mobility projects.
- **Comparative justice frameworks / indices**: emerging in adaptation and sustainability transition research.

### Operationalization ideas
If you want to use temporality as a concept in a framework, the literature suggests six practical dimensions:

1. **Horizon** — what future time frame is used?
2. **Pace** — what is being sped up or slowed down?
3. **Sequence** — what must happen before what?
4. **Duration** — how long do harms, transitions, or delays last?
5. **Synchronization** — whose times are aligned or forced apart?
6. **Responsibility over time** — who bears past harms and future costs?

This makes temporality usable in planning/governance research without reducing it to a single metric.

## 8) Gaps and emerging trends

### Major gaps
- **Europe-specific political ecology of temporality is still thinly consolidated.** Much of the strongest work sits at the boundary of political ecology, STS, planning, and governance.
- **Operational tools remain underdeveloped.** There is growing conceptual clarity but limited standardized measurement of temporal justice or temporal governance.
- **Cross-border and multi-scalar temporal conflicts are under-theorized.** EU-level and borderland temporalities need more direct comparison.
- **More-than-human and deep-time perspectives are still marginal in planning practice.**
- **The temporal politics of restoration, relocation, and adaptation need more empirical work.**

### Emerging trends
- **From speed to synchronization**: moving beyond simple fast/slow binaries.
- **From time as context to time as governance device**: permits, plans, and foresight as temporal instruments.
- **From climate justice to temporal justice**: recognition that justice unfolds across generations and historical harms.
- **From single-scalar studies to multi-scalar and border studies**: especially in infrastructure, migration, and energy networks.
- **From transition targets to lived transition times**: attention to waiting, uncertainty, and uneven pacing.

## 9) Implications for a theoretical framework

A useful framework for your topic can be organized around three linked layers:

### Layer 1: Temporal order
How is time framed?  
Examples: urgency, delay, waiting, long-termism, deep time, transition, restoration.

### Layer 2: Temporal power
Who sets the schedule, horizon, and pace?  
Examples: state, courts, firms, planners, communities, future generations.

### Layer 3: Temporal justice
Who benefits, who bears the burden, and whose futures are protected or foreclosed?  
Examples: intergenerational justice, restoration, relocation, procedural timing, permit time.

That framework is strong enough to connect political ecology with planning and governance while staying empirically usable.

## 10) Bottom line

The literature suggests that temporality is now one of the most promising conceptual bridges between political ecology and European planning/governance research. The strongest common insight is that **time is a governable and contested resource**: it can be compressed, deferred, synchronized, or stretched, and those operations have distributive and ecological consequences. For a theoretical framework, the most productive move is not to ask whether time is fast or slow, but **how temporal relations are organized, by whom, for whose benefit, and with what justice effects**.
